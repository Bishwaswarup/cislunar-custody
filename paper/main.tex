\begin{abstract}
Ground-based optical sensors are the most readily available assets for tracking objects in
cislunar space, but they lose sight of those objects for days at a time. We quantify how
long an object on a cislunar periodic orbit can go unobserved before it leaves a telescope
search pattern, a quantity we call the \emph{custody horizon} $\Tc$. The study covers four
representative orbits of the Earth--Moon system (the 9:2 southern near-rectilinear halo
orbit, an $L_1$ northern halo, an $L_2$ planar Lyapunov orbit and a distant retrograde
orbit) and a four-site network of 1\,m-class telescopes over one year. Seen from Earth,
these targets stay within 4--14$^\circ$ of the Moon. As a result, the Moon-exclusion angle
controls visibility more than telescope aperture does, and every orbit and network suffers a
lunar-phase blackout of 8.5--16\,days. Seven-day tracking arcs reach 1--17\,km position
uncertainty (Cram\'er--Rao bound), which EKF and UKF filters attain. After a 3.2-day gap,
however, the EKF reacquires consistently in only 10\,\% of runs, compared with 100\,\% for
a Gaussian-mixture UKF. Propagating post-fit covariances through gaps of up to 30\,days with
linear, unscented and Monte Carlo methods shows that custody lasts beyond 30\,days for the
NRHO and the DRO. The unstable $L_1$ halo and $L_2$ Lyapunov orbits lose custody after
11--17\,days, depending mainly on how long the object was tracked before the gap: after
1-, 3- and 7-day arcs, 74--89\,\%, 96--100\,\% and 100\,\% of blackouts are survived with a
ten-field search. The horizon is predicted almost exactly (log-$R^2 = 1.00$) by the
unscented transform of the post-fit covariance, using 13 sigma points instead of Monte
Carlo, but not by the orbit's finite-time Lyapunov exponent alone ($R^2 < 0.05$). Linear
(EKF-style) predictions give false custody: after 14 days on the $L_1$ halo their 99\,\%
ellipse contains only 8--16\,\% of the true probability. These conclusions hold within
0.93--1.01 in a DE440 ephemeris model with solar radiation pressure, in which the orbits
are transitioned by multiple shooting.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} cislunar space domain awareness; angles-only orbit determination;
near-rectilinear halo orbit; uncertainty propagation; Gaussian mixture filter; custody

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Traffic beyond geosynchronous orbit is growing: lunar orbiters, the Gateway's 9:2
near-rectilinear halo orbit (NRHO), and spacecraft in libration-point and distant
retrograde orbits. Keeping track of these objects (\emph{custody}) is harder than in near-Earth
space for three reasons. The targets are faint and 380\,000\,km away. Seen from Earth, they
sit close to the brightest object in the night sky. And their dynamics are governed by the
Earth--Moon three-body problem, which stretches uncertainty rapidly and turns it
non-Gaussian \citep{frueh2021}.

Ground-based optical telescopes are the most accessible sensors for this task.
\citet{sechrest2024} detected CAPSTONE at $g \approx 19.6$ and Orion at $g \approx 15.5$ with
36\,cm telescopes. But ground sensors see cislunar objects only intermittently: daylight,
the Moon's glare and the lunar phase open and close observation windows. Earlier work has
shown that filters survive or fail across particular gaps on particular trajectories
\citep{chow2021,iannamorelli2023}. What is missing is a general answer to the operational
question: \emph{how long can a cislunar object go unobserved before it cannot be found
again, and what determines that time?}

This paper answers that question for ground-based, angles-only tracking of periodic orbits.
Its contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A physically generated observation model (sites, twilight, Moon exclusion and
      occultation, shadows, photometry with scattered moonlight). It shows that cislunar
      targets sit within 4--14$^\circ$ of the Moon and that a lunar-phase blackout of
      8.5--16\,days is unavoidable (Section~\ref{sec:vis}).
\item A definition of the \emph{custody horizon} $\Tc$ as the longest gap after which 99\,\%
      of the true probability mass still lies inside a telescope search pattern. We give maps
      of $\Tc$ across orbits, tracking-arc lengths and search sizes (Section~\ref{sec:custody}).
\item Evidence that $\Tc$ can be predicted without Monte Carlo from the stretching of the
      post-fit covariance (unscented transform, log-$R^2 = 1.00$), but not from the orbit's
      finite-time Lyapunov exponent alone (Section~\ref{sec:predict}).
\item A quantification of when the filter choice matters. EKF-style linear prediction gives
      false custody after about one week on unstable orbits, and a Gaussian-mixture UKF
      reacquires reliably where the EKF fails (Sections~\ref{sec:filters} and~\ref{sec:predict}).
\item A cross-check in a DE440 ephemeris model with solar radiation pressure, using
      multiple-shooting ephemeris counterparts of the orbits (Section~\ref{sec:ephem}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\citet{chow2021,chow2022} processed ground-based angles-only and photometric observations
from three sites for 15 periodic orbits. They found that Gaussian-mixture filters retain
custody across data gaps where a UKF fails, and that two-body models lose the object after
the first gap. Their gaps, however, were produced by decimating the data rather than by
physical visibility. \citet{frueh2021} showed that even a global ground network leaves periods
with no visibility because of lunar brightness, and that cislunar uncertainty develops
strongly curved, non-Gaussian structure. For space-based observers, \citet{iannamorelli2023,iannamorelli2024}
kept custody of a manoeuvring Artemis-like target across gaps of 1.7 and 2.6\,days with an
adaptive Gaussian-mixture interacting-multiple-model filter, in 247 of 250 Monte Carlo trials.
For initial orbit determination, \citet{jo2025} developed a three-body differential-correction
method for angles-only data and reviewed the alternatives, including Gauss/Laplace
extensions, particle-swarm, sparse-grid and admissible-region approaches. For observer
placement, \citet{wilmer2022} studied NRHO surveillance from cislunar periodic orbits.

On the estimation side, we build on the unscented transform \citep{julier2004,wan2000},
entropy-triggered Gaussian-mixture splitting \citep{demars2013}, and regularised particle
filtering with progressive correction \citep{oudjane2000,musso2001}. Consistency is assessed
with the normalised estimation error squared (NEES) \citep{barshalom2001}. The dynamical
tools are standard CR3BP methods \citep{szebehely1967,koon2011}: Richardson's third-order
halo approximation \citep{richardson1980}, differential correction and pseudo-arclength
continuation. The finite-time Lyapunov exponent (FTLE) \citep{shadden2005} is the natural
candidate for predicting how fast knowledge decays, and we test it directly.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:models}

\subsection{Dynamics and orbit catalogue}
Motion is modelled in the Earth--Moon circular restricted three-body problem (CR3BP), in
the barycentric rotating frame with the Earth at $(-\mu,0,0)$ and the Moon at $(1-\mu,0,0)$:
\begin{equation}
\ddot x - 2\dot y = \frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial x},\quad
\ddot y + 2\dot x = \frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial y},\quad
\ddot z = \frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial z},\qquad
\Omega = \tfrac12(x^2+y^2) + \frac{1-\mu}{r_1} + \frac{\mu}{r_2},
\end{equation}
with $\mu = 0.0121505842$ (from DE440 gravitational parameters), a length unit of 384\,400\,km and a
time unit of 4.3425\,days. The state transition matrix (STM) $\Phi(t,t_0)$ is integrated
with the variational equations using an eighth-order Runge--Kutta method \citep{hairer1993,virtanen2020}.
It agrees with Richardson-extrapolated finite differences to $3\times10^{-10}$.

Symmetric periodic orbits are computed by single-shooting differential correction and
continued by pseudo-arclength continuation. Halo families are seeded with Richardson's
third-order solution and converge in four iterations. Five families are built: the $L_1$
northern halo (88 members), the $L_2$ southern halo down to the NRHOs (140), the $L_1$ and
$L_2$ Lyapunov families (60 each) and the DRO family (94) (Fig.~\ref{fig:families}). The 9:2
synodic-resonant NRHO is found by period targeting. It has a period of 6.5624\,d, perilune
and apolune radii of 3249 and 71\,222\,km, and a stability index of 1.32, all consistent with
\citet{zimovan2020}. Four representative orbits are used throughout: the 9:2 NRHO, an $L_1$
halo with $A_z \approx 30\,000$\,km, a mid-family $L_2$ Lyapunov orbit, and a DRO whose
closest approach to the Moon is about 70\,000\,km.

\subsection{Sky geometry}
To give the sky geometry real dates, the CR3BP trajectory is placed in the instantaneous
Earth--Moon frame of the actual Moon. The frame's $\hat x$ axis points from Earth to Moon, its
$\hat z$ axis lies along the Moon's orbital angular momentum, and distances scale with the
constant length unit. The Sun and Moon come from low-precision analytic series
\citep{montenbruck2000}, and Earth rotation from GMST. The Moon used in every geometric test
is the mapped CR3BP Moon, so the target--Moon geometry is exactly the CR3BP one. Truth,
filters and predictors share this mapping, so the setup is self-consistent. Section~\ref{sec:ephem}
replaces it with DE440 \citep{park2021}. The reference epoch is 2027-01-01\,UTC and the grid
spacing is 10\,min.

\subsection{Sensors and visibility}
The network consists of Hanle (India), La Palma (Spain), Haleakala (USA) and Siding Spring
(Australia), in subsets of one site (India-1), three sites (Tri-3) and four sites
(Tri-3+S). An observation is possible only if all of the following hold: the Sun is below
$-18^\circ$; the target is above $20^\circ$ elevation; the line of sight misses the Moon; the
target is outside Earth's and the Moon's (cylindrical) shadows; the target is more than
$\theta_{\mathrm{m}}$ from the Moon; and the target is brighter than the effective limiting
magnitude. The target is a Lambertian sphere of radius 1\,m and albedo 0.2 (magnitude
18.4 at zero phase at the Moon's distance). The limiting magnitude is degraded by scattered
moonlight following \citet{krisciunas1991}, and extinction is applied. Dark-sky limiting
magnitudes of 19.5, 21.5 and 23.0 are assumed for 0.36, 1 and 2\,m apertures. The 0.36\,m
value is anchored to the CAPSTONE detection of \citet{sechrest2024}.
Measurements are topocentric right ascension and declination with 1$''$ noise.

\subsection{Estimators}
\label{sec:estimators}
Four filters share one measurement model:
\begin{itemize}
\item an EKF, with STM covariance propagation and Joseph-form updates;
\item a UKF (13 scaled sigma points, $\alpha=1$, $\beta=2$, $\kappa=0$);
\item a Gaussian-mixture UKF (GM-UKF). A component is split when its unscented prediction
      raises the differential entropy of its Gaussian fit by more than 0.02\,nats \citep{demars2013}.
      The flow preserves volume, so any entropy gain comes from nonlinear bending. The split
      is a moment-preserving three-way split along the direction of maximum stretching (the
      top right-singular vector of the statistically linearised flow times the Cholesky factor
      of the covariance). Pruning and Mahalanobis merging keep the mixture small.
\item a regularised bootstrap particle filter with progressive correction
      \citep{oudjane2000,musso2001}. It hands over to a UKF once its position uncertainty
      falls below 50\,km. Without the handover, a finite particle set impoverishes on long, dense
      arcs, and its covariance drops below the Cram\'er--Rao bound.
\end{itemize}
The truth has no process noise. The filters carry a small white acceleration noise with
power spectral density $10^{-18}$\,km$^2$\,s$^{-3}$.

\section{Custody Horizon: Definition and Computation}
\label{sec:custody-def}

Let $(\hat{\vect x}_0, P_0)$ be the post-fit estimate at the last measurement before a
gap. For gap lengths $\Delta t \in [0.1, 30]$\,d, the uncertainty is propagated three ways:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{linear}: $P(\Delta t) = \Phi P_0 \Phi^\top$, which is what an EKF predicts;
\item \emph{unscented} (UT): 13 sigma points, which is what a UKF predicts;
\item \emph{Monte Carlo} (MC): samples of $\mathcal N(\hat{\vect x}_0, P_0)$ through the full
      dynamics, used as the reference.
\end{itemize}
The samples are projected onto the geocentric sky. With a search pattern of $N$ square fields of view of
side $\phi$, whose equal-area radius is $r_N = \phi\sqrt{N/\pi}$, the custody horizon is
\begin{equation}
\Tc(N) = \max\left\{\Delta t : \Pr\left[\angle(\hat{\vect u}_{\mathrm{target}}, \hat{\vect c}) \le r_N\right] \ge 0.99\right\},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\vect c}$ is the centre of the search. Three variants use different centres or
radii:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{ideal}: the circle is centred on the true (MC) mean direction;
\item \emph{actual}: the circle is centred on a method's own predicted direction;
\item \emph{claimed}: the time at which a method's \emph{own} 99\,\% region outgrows $r_N$.
\end{itemize}
A claimed horizon longer than the actual one is \emph{false custody}: the predictor believes
the target is still inside the pattern after it has left. We use $\phi = 1^\circ$ and
$N \in \{1, 10, 100\}$, which give search radii of 0.56$^\circ$, 1.78$^\circ$ and 5.64$^\circ$.
We also report Gaussian \emph{containment}, the share of MC samples inside a method's 99\,\%
sky ellipse (ideal value 99\,\%).

The post-fit covariance at the gap start is the Cram\'er--Rao lower bound of the preceding
tracking arc of 1, 3 or 7\,days:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal I(t_0) = \sum_k \Phi(t_k,t_0)^\top H_k^\top R^{-1} H_k \Phi(t_k,t_0),\qquad
P_0 = \Phi(t_e,t_0)\,\mathcal I^{-1}\,\Phi(t_e,t_0)^\top ,
\end{equation}
built from the actual visible measurements, with the gap starting at the arc's last
measurement. Section~\ref{sec:filters} shows that the filters reach this bound. Two scenarios
are studied: arcs ending every 15\,days through the year (\emph{phase}), and arcs ending
exactly when a blackout of 2 days or more begins (\emph{blackout}). This gives 525 cases.

We also test a purely dynamical predictor: $\hat T_{\mathrm{FTLE}}$ is the first $\Delta t$ at which
the largest singular value of $\Phi$, $s_{\max}(\Phi(\Delta t)) = e^{\sigma_{\Delta t}\Delta t}$,
reaches $r_N/\theta_0$, where $\theta_0$ is the initial 99\,\% sky radius and $\sigma_{\Delta t}$ is the FTLE
\citep{shadden2005}.

\section{Visibility: the Moon and the Lunar Phase Dominate}
\label{sec:vis}

Seen from Earth, the four targets stay close to the Moon (Fig.~\ref{fig:sep}). The $L_1$ halo
stays within 3.9--6.1$^\circ$ and the $L_2$ Lyapunov orbit within 0--7.0$^\circ$. The NRHO ranges
over 0.5--10.0$^\circ$ and spends most of its time near apolune, at about 10$^\circ$. The DRO
ranges over 0--13.9$^\circ$. The Moon-exclusion angle is therefore the dominant sensor
parameter (Table~\ref{tab:vis}). With a 10$^\circ$ exclusion, the $L_1$ and $L_2$ targets are
never observable, whereas at 5$^\circ$ they are observable 21--33\,\% of the year. Doubling
the aperture from 1 to 2\,m changes the visible fraction by only 0.3--1.6 percentage points.

Visibility arrives in windows that run from roughly first quarter to last quarter

\section{Observability and Filter Performance}
\label{sec:filters}

\paragraph{Cram\'er--Rao bounds.} Figure~\ref{fig:crlb} shows the bound, computed from the
actual measurement schedule, for arcs starting every 2 days. At the end of 7-day arcs the
median worst-direction position uncertainty is 1.12\,km (NRHO), 1.74\,km ($L_1$ halo),
6.87\,km (DRO) and 17.1\,km ($L_2$ Lyapunov). The corresponding velocity uncertainties are
6--100\,mm\,s$^{-1}$. Between 75\,\% and 84\,\% of 7-day arcs fully determine the orbit. For
the DRO and the Lyapunov orbit, the weakest direction lies within 7--8$^\circ$ (median) of the
line of sight, i.e.\ it is range. For the NRHO and the halo it is spread more widely
(median 13--18$^\circ$), because the strongly curved dynamics over a week also constrain range.
Arcs that end inside a blackout show the uncertainty growth that motivates the rest of the paper.

\paragraph{Tracking.} When the filters are started at the first measurement of the arc
(from a 1000\,km / 10\,m\,s$^{-1}$ prior), both the EKF and the UKF converge to the bound, as
Table~\ref{tab:filters} shows for 20 Monte Carlo runs per orbit. The averaged NEES is close
to the ideal value of 6. The NRHO's value of 4.4--4.5 is slightly conservative, because the
filters carry process noise that the truth lacks. The EKF begins to lose consistency only on
the unstable orbits.

\paragraph{Reacquisition after a gap.} In the $L_1$ halo arc the first measurement arrives
3.2\,d after the prior epoch. By then the 1000\,km prior has stretched into a curved cloud of
about $40\,000 \times 12\,000$\,km (Fig.~\ref{fig:cloud}). Only 10\,\% of EKF runs end
consistent, with errors of up to 562\,km while the filter claims about 2\,km. The
UKF reaches 70\,\%. The GM-UKF reaches 100\,\% (with a maximum error of 2.4\,km), splitting
into up to 25 components along the stretching direction and averaging 3.1 components over
the arc. The particle filter with UKF handover reaches 70\,\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:reacq}). The
single-Gaussian 99\,\% ellipsoids still contain 96--98\,\% of the particles at the first
measurement. At this gap length the EKF therefore fails mainly in the \emph{measurement
update}, which linearises the angles across a 10$^4$\,km cloud, rather than in the prediction.

\section{Custody Horizons}
\label{sec:custody}

Figure~\ref{fig:growth} shows how the true 99\,\% sky radius grows after 7-day arcs. The
NRHO and DRO stay below 0.01$^\circ$ for 30 days, reflecting their near-stability. The $L_1$
halo and $L_2$ Lyapunov orbits grow roughly exponentially after about 5 days and cross the
search radii at 13--20 days. Because the growth is so steep, enlarging the search from 10 to
100 fields gains only 2--4 days (Table~\ref{tab:tc}). The length of the tracking arc matters
more: the $L_1$ halo's median horizon rises from 10.9 to 16.5\,d (10 fields) as the arc
grows from 1 to 7 days.

\section{Predicting the Custody Horizon, and False Custody}
\label{sec:predict}

Can $\Tc$ be predicted without Monte Carlo? Figure~\ref{fig:predict} compares the Monte Carlo
horizon with two cheap predictors. The \emph{orbit-only} FTLE predictor fails, with log-$R^2$
between $-0.22$ and $0.03$. The \emph{covariance-weighted} predictors succeed. The UT
predictor reaches $R^2 = 0.98$, $1.00$ and $0.86$ for $N = 1$, 10 and 100, and the linear
predictor reaches 0.98, 0.90 and 0.95. The FTLE measures stretching along the flow's most
unstable direction, but the post-fit covariance is highly anisotropic: angles-only data
constrain some directions far better than others, and only the uncertain directions that
project onto the sky matter for the search. Predicting custody requires the growth of the
\emph{actual} covariance, which the unscented transform supplies from 13 sigma points instead
of hundreds of samples.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig13_tc_predictor.png}
\caption{Monte Carlo custody horizon against the orbit-only FTLE predictor (left) and the
UT-predicted covariance (right), for 10 fields.}
\label{fig:predict}
\end{figure}

Linear prediction is adequate for about a week but then breaks down on unstable orbits
(Fig.~\ref{fig:contain}). After 14 days, the $L_1$ halo's linear 99\,\% sky ellipse contains
only 7.6\,\% of the true probability mass (median), while the UT ellipse contains 98\,\%. A
linear predictor used to plan the search would therefore point the telescopes at the wrong
part of the sky. For all orbits the UT never produced false custody at $N = 10$.
Beyond about 15 days, even the UT ellipse loses containment, falling to 70--80\,\%. The
recovery near 30 days is an artefact of the small-angle projection once the distribution
covers tens of degrees, long after custody is lost.

\section{Ephemeris Cross-Check}
\label{sec:ephem}

The CR3BP results are checked in an Earth-centred ephemeris model with DE440 Sun and Moon
point masses \citep{park2021} and cannonball solar radiation pressure (area-to-mass ratio
0.01\,m$^2$\,kg$^{-1}$, $C_r = 1.3$, cylindrical Earth shadow). Each orbit is first
transitioned to its ephemeris counterpart by multiple shooting over 45-day windows. Patch
points are placed every 0.1 periods and corrected by Levenberg--Marquardt followed by
minimum-norm Newton iterations, with STMs from the gravity-gradient variational equations.
Plain minimum-norm Newton diverged, or wandered by up to $10^5$\,km, across NRHO perilune
passages. The $L_1$ halo, $L_2$ Lyapunov and DRO counterparts are continuous to better than
0.4\,m and stay within 3500--5200\,km (median) of their CR3BP shapes. The NRHO counterpart
retains a continuity residual of 50--124\,km, because the corrector stalls in the
ill-conditioned perilune segments. We report this as a limitation. For 60 custody cases
(blackout and phase scenarios, 1- and 7-day arcs), the CRLB covariance is mapped to inertial
coordinates and propagated in the ephemeris model.

The conclusions carry over (Figs.~\ref{fig:ephem} and~\ref{fig:ephemgrowth},
Table~\ref{tab:ephem}). The median ratio of ephemeris to CR3BP horizon is 0.93--1.01, and the
growth curves overlap; 3 of 60 cases are outliers, where local differences in the ephemeris
dynamics shift $\Tc$ by several days. Blackout survival is unchanged except for two
single-case differences within four-case groups. The UT predictor again reaches $R^2 = 1.00$
for $N = 1$ and 10, but only 0.68 for $N = 100$: at a search radius of about 5.6$^\circ$ the
distribution is too curved for any single Gaussian. Linear false custody becomes \emph{worse}.
At 14 days the linear ellipse holds 15.5\,\% of the mass for the $L_1$ halo and 70.8\,\% for
the $L_2$ Lyapunov orbit (98.9\,\% in the CR3BP), while the UT ellipse holds 97--99\,\%.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Operational rules.} Four rules follow from the results.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Sensor design for cislunar tracking from the ground should minimise the usable
      Moon-exclusion angle (baffling, scattered-light rejection) before increasing aperture.
\item The monthly lunar-phase blackout cannot be closed by adding ground sites. Custody
      through it depends on the post-fit covariance, so tasking should concentrate at least
      3--7\,days of tracking before each predicted blackout.
\item Search planning after a gap should use unscented (or Gaussian-mixture) prediction.
      Linear prediction is safe only for gaps shorter than about a week on unstable orbits.
\item Reacquisition after a long gap should use a Gaussian-mixture filter, which costs about
      twice as much as a UKF and was the only filter to reacquire every run.
\end{enumerate}
Because the UT predictor tracks the Monte Carlo horizon with $R^2 = 1.00$ at $N \le 10$, it can be
evaluated on board a tasking scheduler for every catalogued object at negligible cost.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The post-fit covariance is the Cram\'er--Rao bound, which the filters reach in tracking
      but not always immediately after a gap.
\item Sensor parameters (limiting magnitudes, extinction, dark-sky brightness) are nominal.
      The moonlight model of \citet{krisciunas1991} is extrapolated below 10$^\circ$ from the
      Moon, and the exclusion angle stands in for instrument-specific scattered light.
\item Targets are passive, non-manoeuvring 2\,m spheres, and data association and clutter
      are not modelled.
\item 99\,\% quantiles from 300 samples are noisy for individual cases, although the medians
      are stable.
\item The NRHO ephemeris counterpart is not fully continuous.
\item The CR3BP study places orbits in the real Earth--Moon direction with analytic
      ephemerides, and the DE440 check covers 60 of the 525 cases.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Future work.} The main next step is validation with real observations. We plan to
propose observing requests at Indian facilities (the 2\,m HCT at Hanle, the ARIES telescopes
at Devasthal and the GROWTH-India 0.7\,m robotic telescope) for targets and nights selected
from the custody maps. A Sun--Earth $L_2$ halo object such as JWST is a bright proxy target.
Other extensions are custody-aware sensor tasking that uses the UT-predicted $\Tc$, mixed
ground and space-based networks, and manoeuvre detection.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We defined the custody horizon for ground-based, angles-only tracking of cislunar periodic
orbits and mapped it over a year of physically generated observations. Cislunar targets sit
within 4--14$^\circ$ of the Moon, so the exclusion angle and the lunar phase control
visibility, and a blackout of 8.5--16\,days is unavoidable. The near-stable 9:2 NRHO and DRO
keep custody for more than 30 days after three or more days of tracking. The unstable $L_1$
halo and $L_2$ Lyapunov orbits keep it for 11--17\,days (10 fields), with the tracking-arc
length before a gap as the controlling factor. The horizon is predicted almost exactly by the
unscented transform of the post-fit covariance, but not by the orbit's FTLE alone, and linear
prediction gives false custody after about one week on unstable orbits. A Gaussian-mixture UKF
reacquires reliably where the EKF fails. These conclusions hold in a DE440 ephemeris model
with solar radiation pressure.
